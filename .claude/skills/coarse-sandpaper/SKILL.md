# coarse-sandpaper

The draft exists. This pass makes it clear.

## The standard

Strunk's actual position on brevity, which is usually misquoted:

> "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences... This requires not that the writer make all sentences short, or avoid all detail and treat subjects only in outline, but that every word tell."

Every word tells. That is not the same as every sentence being short.

Before editing, read `references/examples.md`. Every rule below has the book's own before/after pairs there. Apply the rules against those examples, not from memory.

## Rules for sentences

1. **Use the active voice.** The actor comes before the action. When a sentence is made stronger it usually gets shorter, so brevity is a by-product of vigor rather than the goal.
2. **Put statements in positive form.** The reader wants to be told what is, not what isn't. Use *not* to deny, never to evade. Save *would, should, could, may, might, can* for real uncertainty.
3. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** The surest way to hold a reader's attention is being specific, definite, and concrete.
4. **Make every word tell.** Cut the words that carry no meaning: *the question as to whether* → *whether*. Cut hedges and empty intensifiers. Keep the connective words — and, so, because, which means — because they are what make writing sound spoken.
5. **Put the emphatic words at the end.** The new information goes last. This applies to the words in a sentence, the sentences in a paragraph, and the paragraphs in a piece.
6. **Keep related words together.** Modifiers sit next to what they modify. Don't separate the subject from its verb with a phrase that could move to the front.
7. **Avoid a run of loose sentences.** Several two-clause sentences in a row, each joined by *and* or *but*, produces singsong. Vary the shape.
8. **Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** Parallel ideas take parallel construction. Uneven forms make the writer look undecided.

## Rules for words

9. **Write with nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs.** No adjective can rescue a weak or inaccurate noun. Nouns and verbs give writing its toughness and color.
10. **Avoid qualifiers.** *Rather, very, little, pretty* — White calls them "the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood of words."
11. **Avoid fancy words.** Take the ten-cent word over the twenty-dollar one. Anglo-Saxon over Latin.
12. **Do not overstate.** A single overstatement makes the reader doubt everything around it. State what happened, at the size it happened.
13. **Prefer the standard to the offbeat.** Established words over jargon, slang, coinages, and business-speak. *Finalize* is White's example: nobody can tell whether it means "terminate" or "put into final form."

## Rules for paragraphs

14. **One topic per paragraph.** The break signals a new step in the argument.
15. **Break up long blocks.** Enormous blocks of print look formidable and readers avoid them. Breaking a long paragraph in two is often a visual help even when the sense doesn't require it. The opposite fault is real too: many short paragraphs in a row read like display advertising.

## Rules that catch cleverness

16. **Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity.** The most common failure in AI writing. It appears as a short, rhythmic sentence at the end of a paragraph that restates the paragraph in fewer words. It reads like a conclusion. It is a performance, and it makes the reader decode what the plain version would have handed them.
17. **Do not overwrite.** Rich, ornate prose is hard to digest. Cut the flowery language even when every word is doing a job.
18. **Do not explain too much.** Say the thing once, completely, then move on. If you made the point and then made it again a second way, cut the second one.
19. **Be clear.** When a sentence is hopelessly mired, start fresh. Usually the construction got too involved and the sentence needs breaking into two.
20. **Write in a way that comes naturally.** Would a person say this out loud to someone they like? If not, rewrite it until they would.
21. **Do not clip words off the front of sentences to sound punchy.** "Took me 3 years" is wrong; "It took me 3 years" is right. Dropped subjects and articles read as choppy performance, not natural speech. Write the full sentence.

22. **Say the thing; never announce it.** The user is talking to one person directly, so start each point on its substance. Delete throat-clearing and meta-commentary: any line that says what you're about to say, why you're saying it, or that a point is coming ("I want to start with where you are, because…", "Here's what that looks like", "There's another piece to this", "Let me walk you through it", "This is why X looks the way it does", "X explains why…:"). "If you're watching this, I want to start with where you are, because I've talked to a lot of people in your seat. Maybe you're still building." → "If you're like most of my clients, you're still building." Why: announcing a point before making it gives every idea three beats instead of one, and the user flagged it as the main reason drafts read weak (Nov 2 2026).

## How to run the pass

1. Read the draft in full before changing anything.
2. Read `references/examples.md`.
3. Go paragraph by paragraph, applying the rules.
4. Read the first and last sentence of every paragraph. Delete the first if it announces the point (rule 22), the last if it restates it.
5. Read the whole draft again. Any sentence you have to read twice gets rewritten or split.

## Precedence

When two rules conflict, clarity wins. If a cut makes a sentence harder to understand on one pass, keep the words.

The voice skill outranks this one. Where a rule here would flatten the author's voice, the voice wins.

Rule 4 never authorizes deleting the author's own connective words. When editing someone's existing writing, their phrasing stays and only genuine filler comes out.

Most drafts get shorter in this pass. Some get longer, because a compressed line needed unpacking. Both are correct. There is no target word count.

## What this pass does not do

It does not add voice, personality, or humor — that is the voice skill. It does not change structure or argument. It does not invent content.
