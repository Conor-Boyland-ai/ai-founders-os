# Examples

Every pair below is from Strunk & White, *The Elements of Style*. These are the book's own examples, not paraphrases. Read them before editing.

---

## 1. Use the active voice

| Weaker | Stronger |
|---|---|
| My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me. | I shall always remember my first visit to Boston. |
| There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground. | Dead leaves covered the ground. |
| At dawn the crowing of a rooster could be heard. | The cock's crow came with dawn. |
| The reason the user left college was that their health became impaired. | Failing health compelled them to leave college. |
| It was not long before she was very sorry that she had said what she had. | She soon repented her words. |

The passive is still right when the thing acted upon is the subject of the paragraph. "The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed today" belongs in a paragraph about those dramatists. "Modern readers have little esteem for the dramatists of the Restoration" belongs in one about modern readers.

## 2. Put statements in positive form

| Weaker | Stronger |
|---|---|
| The user was not very often on time. | The user usually came late. |
| She did not think that studying Latin was a sensible way to use one's time. | She thought the study of Latin a waste of time. |
| not honest | dishonest |
| not important | trifling |
| did not remember | forgot |
| did not pay any attention to | ignored |
| did not have much confidence in | distrusted |
| Plath may be ranked among those modern poets who died young. | Plath was one of those modern poets who died young. |

> "Consciously or unconsciously, the reader is dissatisfied with being told only what is not; the reader wishes to be told what is."

> "If your every sentence admits a doubt, your writing will lack authority."

## 3. Use definite, specific, concrete language

| Vague | Concrete |
|---|---|
| A period of unfavorable weather set in. | It rained every day for a week. |
| The user showed satisfaction as the user took possession of their well-earned reward. | The user grinned as the user pocketed the coin. |
| In proportion as the manners, customs, and amusements of a nation are cruel and barbarous, the regulations of its penal code will be severe. | In proportion as men delight in battles, bullfights, and combats of gladiators, will they punish by hanging, burning, and the rack. |

Orwell's demonstration of strong writing drained of its blood:

| Drained | Original (Ecclesiastes) |
|---|---|
| Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the unpredictable must inevitably be taken into account. | I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favor to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all. |

## 4. Make every word tell

| Wordy | Tight |
|---|---|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| there is no doubt but that | no doubt |
| used for fuel purposes | used for fuel |
| the user is a man who | the user |
| in a hasty manner | hastily |
| this is a subject that | this subject |
| Her story is a strange one. | Her story is strange. |
| the reason why is that | because |
| owing to the fact that | since |
| in spite of the fact that | though |
| call your attention to the fact that | remind you |
| the fact that the user had not succeeded | their failure |
| Their cousin, who is a member of the same firm | Their cousin, a member of the same firm |

*The fact that* should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs.

Wordiness also comes from presenting one idea step by step across several sentences that belong in one:

| 51 words | 26 words |
|---|---|
| Macbeth was very ambitious. This led them to wish to become king of Scotland. The witches told them that this wish of their would come true. The king of Scotland at this time was Duncan. Encouraged by their wife, Macbeth murdered Duncan. The user was thus enabled to succeed Duncan as king. | Encouraged by their wife, Macbeth achieved their ambition and realized the prediction of the witches by murdering Duncan and becoming king of Scotland in their place. |

## 5. Put the emphatic words at the end

| Weaker | Stronger |
|---|---|
| Humanity has hardly advanced in fortitude since that time, though it has advanced in many other ways. | Since that time, humanity has advanced in many ways, but it has hardly advanced in fortitude. |
| This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness. | Because of its hardness, this steel is used principally for making razors. |

The other emphatic position is the beginning. Any element other than the subject becomes emphatic when placed first: "Deceit or treachery she could never forgive."

## 6. Keep related words together

| Confusing | Clear |
|---|---|
| The user noticed a large stain in the rug that was right in the center. | The user noticed a large stain right in the center of the rug. |
| You can call your mother in London and tell her all about George's taking you out to dinner for just two dollars. | For just two dollars you can call your mother in London and tell her all about George's taking you out to dinner. |
| Toni Morrison, in Beloved, writes about characters who have escaped from slavery but are haunted by its heritage. | In Beloved, Toni Morrison writes about characters who have escaped from slavery but are haunted by its heritage. |
| A dog, if you fail to discipline them, becomes a household pest. | Unless disciplined, a dog becomes a household pest. |
| All the members were not present. | Not all the members were present. |
| She only found two mistakes. | She found only two mistakes. |

## 7. Avoid a run of loose sentences

The fault, in White's example:

> "The third concert of the subscription series was given last evening, and a large audience was in attendance. Mr. Edward Appleton was the soloist, and the Boston Symphony Orchestra furnished the instrumental music. The former showed themselves to be an artist of the first rank, while the latter proved itself fully deserving of its high reputation."

> "Apart from its triteness and emptiness, the paragraph above is bad because of the structure of its sentences, with their mechanical symmetry and singsong."

The fix is to recast enough of them to break the pattern — simple sentences, two clauses joined by a semicolon, periodic sentences, or sentences of three clauses.

## 8. Express coordinate ideas in similar form

| Uneven | Parallel |
|---|---|
| Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method, while now the laboratory method is employed. | Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught by the laboratory method. |
| the French, the Italians, Spanish, and Portuguese | the French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese |
| It was both a long ceremony and very tedious. | The ceremony was both long and tedious. |
| A time not for words but action. | A time not for words but for action. |
| Either you must grant their request or incur their ill will. | You must either grant their request or incur their ill will. |

> "The lefthand version gives the impression that the writer is undecided or timid, apparently unable or afraid to choose one form of expression and hold to it."

## 9. Write with nouns and verbs

> "The adjective hasn't been built that can pull a weak or inaccurate noun out of a tight place."

Adjectives and adverbs aren't banned. They occasionally surprise us with their power:

> Up the airy mountain,
> Down the rushy glen,
> We daren't go a-hunting
> For fear of little men...

> "The nouns *mountain* and *glen* are accurate enough, but had the mountain not become airy, the glen rushy, William Allingham might never have got off the ground with their poem. In general, however, it is nouns and verbs, not their assistants, that give good writing its toughness and color."

## 10. Avoid qualifiers

> "Rather, very, little, pretty — these are the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood of words. The constant use of the adjective *little* (except to indicate size) is particularly debilitating; we should all try to do a little better, we should all be very watchful of this rule, for it is a rather important one, and we are pretty sure to violate it now and then."

That last sentence is White demonstrating the fault while naming it. Count the qualifiers.

## 11. Avoid fancy words

> "Avoid the elaborate, the pretentious, the coy, and the cute. Do not be tempted by a twenty-dollar word when there is a ten-center handy, ready and able. Anglo-Saxon is a livelier tongue than Latin, so use Anglo-Saxon words."

> "If you admire fancy words, if every sky is beauteous, every blonde curvaceous, every intelligent child prodigious, if you are tickled by discombobulate, you will have a bad time with Reminder 14."

> "Never call a stomach a tummy without good reason."

## 12. Do not overstate

> "When you overstate, readers will be instantly on guard, and everything that has preceded your overstatement as well as everything that follows it will be suspect in their minds because they have lost confidence in your judgment or your poise. A single overstatement, wherever or however it occurs, diminishes the whole, and a single carefree superlative has the power to destroy, for readers, the object of your enthusiasm."

## 13. Prefer the standard to the offbeat

White's examples of the offbeat: *finalize, accessorize, obsoletes,* and *-wise* endings like *taxwise* or *pricewise*.

> "*Finalize*, for instance, is not standard; it is special, and it is a peculiarly fuzzy and silly word. Does it mean 'terminate,' or does it mean 'put into final form'? One can't be sure, really, what it means, and one gets the impression that the person using it doesn't know, either, and doesn't want to know."

On advertising language:

> "Your new kitchen range is so revolutionary it obsoletes all other ranges. Your counter top is beautiful because it is accessorized with gold-plated faucets... You do so at your peril, for it is the language of mutilation."

## 14–15. Paragraphs

> "The beginning of each paragraph is a signal that a new step in the development of the subject has been reached."

> "Enormous blocks of print look formidable to readers, who are often reluctant to tackle them. Therefore, breaking long paragraphs in two, even if it is not necessary to do so for sense, meaning, or logical development, is often a visual help."

> "But remember, too, that firing off many short paragraphs in quick succession can be distracting. Paragraph breaks used only for show read like the writing of commerce or of display advertising."

## 16. Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity

> "Many shortcuts are self-defeating; they waste the reader's time instead of conserving it. There are all sorts of rhetorical stratagems and devices that attract writers who hope to be pithy, but most of them are simply bothersome. The longest way round is usually the shortest way home, and the one truly reliable shortcut in writing is to choose words that are strong and surefooted to carry readers on their way."

This is the rule AI writing fails most often. It shows up as a short, rhythmic sentence at the end of a paragraph that says what the paragraph already said. It reads like a conclusion. It is a performance, and it makes the reader decode something the plain version would have handed them.

| Compressed (the fault) | Plain (the fix) |
|---|---|
| If the product already exists, every realization costs you a rebuild. So you either eat the work or you talk yourself out of the better idea. Write first and those changes are free. | During the writing process, you almost always realize there is a stronger and clearer way to present your offer, and you need to be flexible enough to change anything you need to change. |
| Read the whole draft before you say anything. You're looking for sections that drag or land in the wrong order, not typos. The big stuff first, the small stuff later. | Read the whole draft before you say anything. You're looking for sections that drag or land in the wrong order, not typos. |
| Your agent gets you 80 to 90 percent of the way there. The last stretch is you. | Your agent gets you 80 to 90 percent of the way there, and the last stretch is the part you do yourself. |

In each pair, the left version is shorter and worse. The reader has to unpack a metaphor or infer a connection the plain version states. The test: read the last sentence of a paragraph and ask whether it adds information or repeats the paragraph in fewer words. If it repeats, delete it.

## 17. Do not overwrite

> "Rich, ornate prose is hard to digest, generally unwholesome, and sometimes nauseating."

> "It is always a good idea to reread your writing later and ruthlessly delete the excess."

## 18. Do not explain too much

> "It is seldom advisable to tell all."

White's example is dialogue attribution: "the user said consolingly," "she replied grumblingly." The adverb is the writer explaining what the line already showed.

> "Inexperienced writers not only overwork their adverbs but load their attributives with explanatory verbs: 'the user consoled,' 'she congratulated.' They do this, apparently, in the belief that the word *said* is always in need of support."

## 19. Be clear

> "Clarity, clarity, clarity. When you become hopelessly mired in a sentence, it is best to start fresh; do not try to fight your way through against the terrible odds of syntax. Usually what is wrong is that the construction has become too involved at some point; the sentence needs to be broken apart and replaced by two or more shorter sentences."

> "When you say something, make sure you have said it. The chances of your having said it are only fair."

## 20. Write in a way that comes naturally

> "Write in a way that comes easily and naturally to you, using words and phrases that come readily to hand. But do not assume that because you have acted naturally your product is without flaw."

> "The approach to style is by way of plainness, simplicity, orderliness, sincerity."
